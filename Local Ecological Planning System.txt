So I would sharpen NILA into a more specific invention:
NILA: A Constraint-First Ecological City Design Engine
The key is that the system must not merely score a design after it is generated. It must use verified ecological constraints during generation, then independently verify the result.
Stage 1 — Understand the site
Input: site boundary, existing land use, terrain, waterways, vegetation, ecological data and development needs.
Stage 2 — Compile ecological constraints
Classify areas as protected, conditionally developable or uncertain. Convert verified rules into spatial constraints.
Stage 3 — Generate multiple layouts
Optimize building placement, roads, green corridors and services subject to ecological and planning constraints.
Stage 4 — Independently verify
Reject layouts that violate hard constraints. Evaluate habitat connectivity, water-related indicators, heat exposure and housing capacity.
Stage 5 — Compare the Pareto frontier
Present the best feasible alternatives and explain trade-offs instead of pretending one design is perfect in every dimension.
The term Pareto frontier means a collection of designs where improving one objective generally requires sacrificing another. For example, one design might support more housing while another preserves more connected green space.
This is a much more defensible engineering contribution than asking a language model to draw a sustainable city.

4. Your research claim and evaluation
Use this as your proposed research hypothesis:
A constraint-first generative planning approach can reduce ecological constraint violations while retaining acceptable housing capacity and improving selected ecological indicators compared with an unconstrained baseline.
Test it using the same study area and development requirements for both approaches.
Measure:
* Number of hard ecological constraint violations.
* Housing capacity and land-use feasibility.
* Green-space connectivity.
* Estimated runoff or heat indicators, where suitable models and data are available.
* Runtime and percentage of generated layouts that pass independent validation.
Do not report improvements until you have actually run the experiments. If the prototype uses synthetic data, clearly identify the results as synthetic.
5. Your winning presentation pitch
Cities are usually designed around buildings, roads, economic growth and infrastructure. Nature is often considered afterward, when damage has already occurred.
Our project, NILA, proposes a different approach: designing cities around ecological constraints from the beginning.
NILA combines geospatial intelligence, constraint-based generation and environmental scenario evaluation to create and compare alternative urban layouts.
Its key innovation is a constraint-first design process. Rather than generating a city and simply assigning it a sustainability score, the system incorporates verified ecological restrictions into layout generation and independently checks whether the resulting designs comply.
We are starting with Odisha, where the system can be tested against a real urban-planning context, and designing the underlying architecture to be reusable internationally.
Our long-term vision is to help planners answer a fundamental question before construction begins: can we meet human needs while avoiding preventable damage to the living systems on which cities depend?
NILA is not a claim that zero ecological impact can already be guaranteed. It is a research-driven attempt to make ecological protection measurable, enforceable within the design process, and continuously verifiable.
